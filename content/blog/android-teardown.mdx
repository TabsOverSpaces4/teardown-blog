I didn't switch to Android; I just started there. Eight years ago I picked up my first Samsung Galaxy because I wanted control over my phone, not simplicity or prestige, just the freedom to make it work the way I wanted it to, and I've stayed ever since.

There was no big revelation or defining moment where I thought this is the one. I just picked a platform, stuck with it, and it kept earning its place.

Worth mentioning though: I'm not purely Android, since I've had an iPad for years and still use it, which means I actually know what the Apple side looks like, at least on the tablet end. But the phone has stayed Android the whole time, and that probably says more than anything else I could write here.

## The freedom is real

I picked Android for customization, and eight years later that still holds.

My home screen looks the way I want it to look, default apps are whatever I decide, and the file system is right there when I need it. Widgets, launchers, automation routines, the tools are all there and they actually work, and while none of this surprises Android users, if you've spent time on iOS you know these are things Apple still resists letting you do.

Android rewards people who put in the time, and if you actually want to build a setup that fits how you work and think, this platform will let you do exactly that. If you just want something that works straight out of the box and never have to think about it again, you'll be fine too, but you'll miss a lot of what makes the platform genuinely good.

<Callout type="tip">If you're new to Android customization, start with a launcher like Nova or Niagara and a simple Tasker routine — the learning curve is real, but even small tweaks can make a surprising difference in how your phone feels to use.</Callout>

## Few years later, it still runs

Longevity is the thing I don't hear talked about enough, and I'm not sure why.

I have a friend who switched to iPhone around the same time I got my first Galaxy, and we both kept our phones for a while before the difference became hard to ignore. A few years in, her iPhone had slowed down noticeably and the battery was basically gone, while mine was still running fast.

A big part of that comes down to how Android handles battery management, with systems built in that track and manage charge cycles in a way that genuinely extends battery life over time. It's not exciting stuff to talk about, but when you're on year three or four of a phone, it starts to matter a great deal.

Fast charging is another feature that sounds minor until you actually depend on it. I'm out of the house a lot, and being able to plug in for fifteen minutes and have meaningful battery back has become one of those things I don't think about much precisely because it just works, and I'd really miss it if it were gone.

<Callout type="info">Samsung's Adaptive Battery system uses on-device machine learning to study your usage patterns and limit power to apps you rarely use. Combined with their cap-at-85% charging option, it's designed to keep battery health high well past the two-year mark.</Callout>

## Durability. Seriously.

I drop things a lot, and my phone has hit the floor more times than I want to admit, ending up in situations where it really had no business surviving.

And it has survived all of them.

The hardware is genuinely well built, and you can find YouTube videos of drop tests and water submersion tests where it holds up in ways that seem almost unreasonable, which has matched my real life experience pretty closely. Once you've owned a phone that cracked on a bad drop, you stop taking that kind of thing for granted.

## The repair service gap nobody talks about

I had to bring my phone in for repair once, and they fixed it on the spot.

Not in a few days or "we'll call you when it's ready," but right there while I waited. I walked in with a broken phone and left with a working one.

Nobody really talks about this when comparing phones, but it's a genuinely important part of the ownership experience since something will go wrong eventually and how that gets handled really matters. From what I've seen, the service experience on the Android side is meaningfully better than what Apple offers: faster, more direct, and considerably less painful.

## The ecosystem question (and my iPad)

This is the part where things get a little messy.

I've been using an iPad for years, which puts me in the slightly awkward position of having an Android phone and an Apple tablet, and while that sounds like a mess on paper, in practice it's fine most of the time. Google Drive handles most of the heavy lifting and I've built a workflow that doesn't constantly run into walls.

But mostly fine isn't the same as smooth, because file transfers that would take two seconds between Apple devices need an extra step, AirDrop just doesn't work across platforms, and whenever friends share something over iMessage I'm the green bubble. They're small things individually, but they do add up.

What's interesting is that my reaction to all this friction isn't to think about switching my phone to an iPhone, but rather to think about switching my iPad to a Samsung tablet, where the service would be better, the OS would feel more natural to me at this point, and I'd have one fewer workaround to manage. The Apple ecosystem is supposed to pull you in deeper, but it's not really working on me.

<Callout type="warning">If you're running a split ecosystem like this, lean on platform-agnostic tools — Google Drive, Notion, Spotify, browser-based apps — for anything you need on both devices. The moment you rely on an Apple-only or Android-only feature for a core workflow, the friction gets much worse.</Callout>

## AI has arrived, and it's actually good

When I opened my current phone for the first time, Gemini was already on it, not tucked away somewhere in settings but right there on the home screen.

I've been watching what both platforms are doing with AI for a while now, and while Siri has had plenty of time to get better and remains just kind of there, Gemini on Android is a genuinely different situation. Combined with the other AI features that come built in, it's something I actually use on a regular basis, whether for summarizing things, translating, or just asking questions conversationally, and it's not a feature I set up once and forgot about.

Things are moving fast in this space and I won't pretend to know where it all lands in two years, but right now Android is not behind, and on older devices Apple Intelligence doesn't even run at all, which is a real and meaningful gap.

## The part I'll be honest about: apps

The honest weak spot isn't the operating system, it's the apps.

Some apps still show up on Android later, and some feel like they were designed for iPhone and ported over without much care, and while the gap has gotten smaller over the years it genuinely hasn't gone away. Certain apps, especially creative ones, are just cleaner and more complete on iOS, and the camera inside Instagram, for example, still looks noticeably better on iPhone in a way that you can actually tell.

For most things I use day to day the apps are perfectly fine, but it would be dishonest to pretend that gap doesn't exist in certain areas.

## The price you actually pay

Android covers a huge range of prices, and you can spend the same as an iPhone and get hardware that genuinely competes with it, or you can spend quite a bit less and still end up with something solid.

Apple doesn't really offer that kind of range, and as the starting price keeps going up, part of what you're paying for is just the name. Android has always had the better value side of this conversation, and that's still true.

The interesting thing about the high end Android phones now is that they genuinely match Apple on specs while still giving you everything that makes Android what it is, whereas a few years ago there was a real tradeoff between performance and customization that just doesn't exist anymore.

<Callout type="info">Samsung's flagship Galaxy S series typically launches at a lower starting price than the equivalent iPhone Pro, and mid-range options like the Galaxy A series consistently rank among the best-value smartphones available.</Callout>

## The Verdict

Still here. Still Android.

Eight years is a long time, and there's definitely some inertia in that. Eight years of contacts, preferences, apps, and built up habits is genuinely hard to leave behind, but I don't stay out of laziness. I stay because when I actually think it through, it still makes sense for me.

The phone works the way I want it to, the battery still holds up, when something broke they fixed it immediately, the AI features are genuinely good and getting better, and I didn't have to pay a premium to get any of it.

The frustrations are real and I'm not pretending otherwise. Some apps aren't as good, hanging out with iPhone friends has its moments of friction, and I'm aware I'm not in the bigger ecosystem, but none of that has been enough to make me actually leave.

If anything, I'm increasingly thinking about making my iPad an Android tablet too, since it would be simpler, more consistent, and the service would be better. But the phone isn't going anywhere regardless, not because I've run out of options or have nowhere else to go, but because after eight years it's still genuinely the one I'd choose.
