I switched from Android to iOS about two years ago. Not because of some grand revelation. I just bought a MacBook, then an Apple Watch, and at some point the iPhone became the obvious missing piece. I'm still on the iPhone 14 Pro, which is now nearly four generations old. And honestly? It still runs like it did on day one.

That surprised me more than anything else about this platform.

## The optimization is real

The thing that hit me first when I moved from Android was how *deliberate* iOS feels. Every element of the OS seems like it's there for a reason. Nothing feels like bloatware. Nothing feels like it was bolted on by a different team with different priorities.

Face ID has gotten better with each software update. The Dynamic Island, which I thought was a gimmick, has quietly become useful as more apps support it. The animations are smooth. The interface is clean. There's a lack of noise and stutters that I didn't fully appreciate until I stopped experiencing them.

Even the cameras still hold up. I'm not just talking about the camera app. I mean the cameras *inside* other apps. Instagram, Snapchat, the usual suspects. iOS still has a meaningful edge here over Android, and if you're someone who uses those platforms regularly, you feel it.

<Callout type="tip">Even on the iPhone 14 Pro, nearly four generations later, iOS camera processing inside third-party apps like Instagram and Snapchat remains noticeably better than most Android flagships. This is one of those things you don't notice until you compare side by side.</Callout>

## "Pro" doesn't mean what it should

Here's where I start to push back. I bought the Pro model. I expected *professional* features. What I got was an extra camera lens, slightly better battery life, and a faster refresh rate.

That's fine, but it's not *pro*. Where are the advanced camera controls baked into the native app? Where's the deeper customization for power users: button remapping, interface tweaks, workflow shortcuts that go beyond what Shortcuts can do?

There are third-party apps that try to fill these gaps, and some of them are genuinely good. But they never feel quite right. iOS has this fluency to its interface, this way everything flows together that's hard to describe until you use an app that doesn't match it. Third-party solutions always feel slightly out of place, like a well-made aftermarket part in an otherwise factory car.

<Callout type="warning">If you're buying the "Pro" model expecting a meaningfully different software experience from the base iPhone, temper those expectations. The differences are almost entirely hardware-level: an extra lens, ProMotion display, and better battery. The software is identical.</Callout>

## Notifications are still a mess

I need to talk about this because it's been two years and I still can't make peace with it.

If I don't dismiss a notification, it sits in my tray. Forever. I have notifications that are two or three weeks old that occasionally surface and give me a small heart attack, making me think I missed something important. The system has gotten cleaner over time, I'll give it that, but "cleaner" and "good" aren't the same thing. Android handled this better. I'll just say it.

## Liquid Glass isn't for me

The latest iOS update introduced Liquid Glass, and I'm not a fan. It's visually impressive in isolation, but it's graphically heavy and I don't love the idea of my gyroscope running constantly so I can watch reflections shift every time I tilt my phone. There are pieces of it I appreciate (some of the UI elements look genuinely elegant), but the overall effect feels like it's optimizing for demo videos rather than daily use.

<Callout type="info">Liquid Glass is Apple's new design language introduced in the latest iOS update. It uses translucent, reflective UI elements that respond to device orientation via the gyroscope, creating a dynamic glass-like effect across the interface.</Callout>

## The ecosystem trap is real (and I'm in it)

I'll be honest about why I'm still here: at least 35% of the reason is that I own a MacBook and an Apple Watch. The "walled garden" isn't just a talking point. It's a financial reality. Switching to Android at this point would mean replacing multiple devices, re-buying apps, and rebuilding workflows. That's not a small ask.

Apple used to have an even stronger lock-in through AirDrop and iMessage. But Android getting RCS support, plus UK regulations pushing Apple to open up AirDrop, has narrowed that gap meaningfully. The moat is still there. It's just not as wide as it was.

## The small wins that matter

A few things deserve credit because they've quietly made the phone better.

Call screening is now built in, which is genuinely great. Integrated voicemail support in the recent update was a welcome change. The widgets have gotten more useful with each release. And the Shortcuts app, when it works, allows for some solid native automation.

I'm intentionally not talking about Siri. You already know.

## The price you pay (literally)

One thing that's bothered me recently: there's growing evidence that certain commercial apps charge more when you order from an iOS device compared to Android or Windows. That's frustrating, especially since the price gap between Android flagships and iPhones has shrunk considerably. You're already paying a premium for the hardware. Getting quietly upcharged on top of that doesn't sit well.

## Where AI fits (or doesn't)

iOS is behind on AI. I know it, Apple knows it, and if you're on an older device like mine, you're locked out of Apple Intelligence entirely. I can live with that (the phone still does everything else well), but it's worth acknowledging. If AI integration is a priority for you, iOS isn't leading that race right now. Whether Apple catches up in a meaningful way remains to be seen.

## So where does that leave me?

iOS is good. Not perfect, not revolutionary at this point, but consistently good. My iPhone 14 Pro still performs well, the battery health is solid, and I genuinely enjoy using it day to day. I use it without a case because the build quality deserves to be felt, not hidden.

The things I love (the optimization, the animations, the app ecosystem, the hardware-software integration) still outweigh the things that frustrate me. And the things that frustrate me (the notification system, the lack of real "pro" features, the AI gap) aren't dealbreakers. They're just reminders that no platform has this figured out completely.

I'll stick with iOS. Not out of loyalty, not out of excitement. Out of the quiet confidence that it'll keep working exactly as well tomorrow as it does today. For a phone, that might be the highest compliment I can give.
