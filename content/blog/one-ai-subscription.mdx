Twenty dollars a month. Where I live, that's two coffees, maybe three if I order badly. It's also what Claude Pro costs me, and it's the only AI subscription that comes out of my own pocket. That fact alone says something, and I want to unpack why the math works, because on paper it shouldn't.

## The stack

I run four AI tools daily: Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, and ChatGPT. I have Pro versions of the first three, but Gemini comes free with my student account, so the only AI line item on my bank statement is Claude.

Being a heavy user, I've made my entire process almost too easy at this point. Wispr Flow handles anything I need to write or type, and it's been flawless. Gemini takes image and video generation, plus anything that needs a more creative hand. Claude gets everything else: writing, coding, automations, and the API credits that power my experiments.

That division of labor took a while to settle. Claude ended up with the biggest share for a reason.

## What twenty dollars actually buys

Here's the honest inventory of what I'm using right now:

- **Claude Design** makes presentations, website designs, wireframes, or even raw sketches of whatever I'm trying to end up with. It's the fastest way I know to go from "vague idea" to "thing I can react to."
- **Cowork** handles my automations and turns scattered tasks into an actual flow. It's the feature I point to when someone asks what "agentic" means in practice.
- **Claude in my browser** automates the repetitive stuff I used to do by hand. I'll be honest: this one still feels a little heavy on my token usage, so I use it with intention rather than by default.
- **Claude Code** lives in my terminal and might be the single most important tool in my setup. I've gotten comfortable running multiple agents at once, and yes, I've had my dangerously-skip-permissions moments that I would not recommend to anyone but also won't apologize for. Commands like context, compact, rewind, and hooks have gone from documentation entries to muscle memory. I've even learned when to stop obsessing over the project files and just let it work.

On top of all that, it plugs into my calendar, Slack, and Todoist, so a whole layer of my day is now something I can control from one place instead of six tabs.

## The quiet feature nobody talks about

The most underrated thing in the whole subscription is Projects. I keep separate projects for separate parts of my life, each with its own context and its own standing instructions.

There's one in my account called Blog Writer. It holds my voice, my format, and my rules for how I write. I drop my raw thoughts in, and it reshapes them in my language without me pasting the same instructions for the hundredth time. You're reading its output right now, which is probably the most honest product endorsement I can give.

## The novelty factor

There's one more reason I pay, and I'm not going to pretend it's practical: I get the latest and most powerful models the moment they land. Fable 5 came out recently and it's been excellent.

Testing new models myself means I actually understand what in the AI world is hype and what is not, instead of taking someone's word for it. I find genuine pleasure in that, and I don't think that's wrong. Curiosity is allowed to be part of the value.

## So, would I go free?

No. Never, honestly.

I want to be fair about this: these tools are purely economical if you have the use for them. If I weren't coding, didn't need projects, and had nothing to automate, I could live on free tiers and just spend a few extra minutes per prompt. Plenty of people should do exactly that.

But I do have the use, and those twenty dollars buy me something harder to measure: peace of mind. Of every subscription I hold, this is the one I'm getting the most out of.

And if the trade is my peace of mind or two coffees a month? I'll drink at home.
